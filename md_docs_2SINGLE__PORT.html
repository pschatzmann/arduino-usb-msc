One USB port: drive, power and Serial (host mode)

In host mode the board's native USB port talks to the drive, so it can no longer be used for the PC connection. That raises three questions:

  1. How does the drive get its 5 V?
  2. How is the board powered?
  3. Where does Serial go (and how do I flash)?

‍The wiring below is a guide, not a tested reference design. Check your board's schematic before connecting power, and never join two 5 V sources without a diode or a proper power-path chip.

The easy way: a board with two USB connectors

Pick a board that separates the roles:

Role Connector
Power, flashing, Serial the USB-to-UART port (e.g. "UART" on an ESP32-S3-DevKitC-1)
Drive the native USB / OTG port (via a USB-OTG adapter or a Type-A socket)

Other examples: Adafruit Feather RP2040 USB Host (Type-A host socket plus a Type-C port for power and programming), ESP32-S3 boards with a Type-A host socket. Two things to check:

  • Does 5 V reach the OTG connector's VBUS? On some boards the second connector is not connected to 5 V by default (a solder jumper or pad may have to be bridged). With no 5 V on VBUS the drive stays dead and nothing enumerates.
  • A flash drive draws roughly 100-500 mA. The supply behind the UART port must be able to deliver that on top of the board itself.

Boards with a single USB port

Only the native USB port exists, and it belongs to the drive. Split the jobs:

(phone │
charger, ├─────────────► USB-A socket pin 1 (VBUS)
power bank) │
GND ─────────┼─────────────► board GND
└─────────────► USB-A socket pin 4 (GND)
USB-A socket pin 2 (D-) ◄── board USB D-
USB-A socket pin 3 (D+) ◄── board USB D+
USB-UART adapter (3.3 V logic): its RX ◄── board TX, its TX ──► board RX, GND common
  • Drive: wire a USB-A receptacle (breakout board) to the chip's USB data pins and a 5 V supply. D+/D- pins:
    • ESP32-S2/S3: D- = GPIO19, D+ = GPIO20
    • RP2040: the USB pads on the board (or use a board with a host socket)
    • STM32F4/F7: D- = PA11, D+ = PA12
  • Power: feed 5 V into the board's 5V/VIN pin from a charger or power bank, and use the same supply for the socket's VBUS. Keep wires short and add a bulk capacitor (about 100 µF) near the socket so the drive's inrush doesn't brown out the board.
  • Do not also plug the board's own USB connector into a PC while the socket is wired to the same pins: both would drive D+/D- and VBUS.
  • Alternative to a bare socket: a USB-OTG "Y" cable or adapter with a separate power input. Plug its host end into the board's USB connector, the drive into its socket, and power into its power input. Whether the extra power reaches the board's 5 V depends on the board's connector wiring, so verify with a multimeter first.

Using a powered OTG hub

A self-powered hub (one with its own 5 V input) plugged into the board's USB port is the simplest way to get the drive's power right:

  • The hub supplies VBUS and the drive's current, so the board's connector doesn't have to. This also helps boards whose OTG connector carries no 5 V.
  • You can plug in several drives; use USBMscFSPool to mount them all (see MULTIPLE_DEVICES.md).
  • ESP32-S2/S3 and RP2040 host at full speed (12 Mbit/s). A plain USB 2.0 hub works; a USB 3 hub should fall back to USB 2.

What a hub does not do:

  • It doesn't give you a PC connection. A hub has one upstream port and that one belongs to the board; a PC is a second host and cannot sit downstream of it. On these chips the OTG controller also shares its USB PHY with the USB-serial/JTAG function (and, in "USB-OTG (TinyUSB)" mode, with the TinyUSB device stack), so the board can't be a host and a PC-visible device at the same time. Serial still has to go over a UART adapter or Wi-Fi (next section). A USB-to-serial dongle on the hub doesn't help either: its other side is UART pins, not your PC.
  • It doesn't reliably power the board. The USB spec forbids a hub from feeding power upstream, though some cheap hubs do. Give the board its own 5 V input.

Getting <tt>Serial</tt> back

Serial normally means the USB port, which is now busy. Use a hardware UART instead and a USB-UART adapter (CP2102, CH340, FT232, ...) between the UART pins and your PC:

  • ESP32-S2/S3: set Tools > USB Mode > USB-OTG (TinyUSB) (required for the host stack) and leave USB CDC On Boot > Disabled. Serial is then UART0: TX = GPIO43, RX = GPIO44. Or open any UART explicitly: Serial1.begin(115200, SERIAL_8N1, /*rx=*/44, /*tx=*/43);
  • RP2040: Serial1 on GP0 (TX) / GP1 (RX); the examples already use Serial1 there.
  • STM32: Serial1 on its UART pins; the examples already use it.

Connect adapter RX to board TX, adapter TX to board RX, and the grounds. Keep the adapter at 3.3 V logic. If the adapter powers the board too (some have a 5 V pin), don't connect that pin as well as the separate supply.

No UART adapter handy? Other ways to see output:

  • Log over Wi-Fi (UDP/telnet) on ESP32.
  • Write results to a file on the drive itself (the filesystem examples already show how) and read it on your PC afterwards.
  • Blink an LED / drive a display for pass/fail.

Flashing

  • ESP32-S3 over the native port: with the drive unplugged, hold BOOT, tap RESET, release BOOT. The chip appears as a serial device and you can upload. Afterwards switch to the drive wiring. Host mode disables the USB serial/JTAG function, so repeat the BOOT+RESET steps for each re-flash.
  • ESP32-S3 over UART: the adapter's DTR/RTS lines on EN and GPIO0 give automatic reset into download mode; otherwise use the same BOOT+RESET dance. This avoids touching the drive wiring.
  • RP2040: hold BOOTSEL while powering up and drag the UF2 (or let the IDE upload) – the host port isn't involved.
  • STM32: flash over the ST-Link/SWD header, or the UART bootloader.

Checklist when nothing happens

  1. Meter between the drive socket's VBUS and GND: about 5 V?
  2. D+/D- not swapped, and the drive not behind a hub that needs more current than the supply gives.
  3. Board configured per BOARDS.md (host mode, patches).
  4. Serial really points at the UART you've wired up.
  5. Try a different drive: some drives need more current than others, or take a long time to spin up.
